UniBank

A dual-sided banking concept built to help institutions serve the families that traditional banking treats as edge cases.

UniBank is a hyper-personalized banking system that adapts to how a household actually works, with a consumer app for families and a software platform for the advisors who serve them.

Scope

Partner

the gap

Banking still assumes one kind of family

Traditional banking assumes one household shape: two married parents, shared everything, and steady income. Plenty of families do not look like that. Single parents, same-sex and blended families, cohabiting partners, and grandparent-led homes all fall outside the template, and their money does not behave the way standard products expect.

We set out to design for those households. Speaking to both sides of the relationship, the families themselves and the advisors who serve them, we mapped where the current experience breaks down.

Families

Income that does not sit still

When income varies or arrives unevenly across a household, budgeting tools built around a single steady paycheque break immediately.

Splitting without a system

Shared costs rarely divide down the middle. Without a framework, households calculate splits by hand, chase transfers, and track who paid what in spreadsheets.

Products built for someone else

Mortgages, planning tools, and paperwork assume a married two-parent structure, leaving families to work around gaps the product never anticipated.

Scattered across institutions

Money lives in different accounts at different banks, with no single view of what the household actually has.

Institutions

Context built by hand

Advisors spend the opening of every conversation reconstructing how a household is structured before any advice can begin.

One-size-fits-all offerings

Generic products convert poorly with households they were never designed around.

A segment left on the table

Non-traditional families are a large and growing group, and most institutions have no clear approach for them.

the approach

One system, built to adapt

Two key improvements we wanted to make:

  1. Make the product adapt to the household, so families stop doing manual work the bank should be doing for them.
  2. Give advisors the context and tools to serve those households well, so the human side of banking scales.

We built the solution on three guiding principles:

Adaptive by default
The product shapes itself around the household instead of asking the household to fit the product.
Clarity in shared money
When money is shared, everyone should see the same clear picture of who owes what, and when.
AI with a human beside it
Automation handles insight and admin, while a real advisor stays within reach for the decisions that matter.

That led us to a two-sided system: a consumer app that builds itself around each family’s profile, and an advisor platform that gives institutions the same picture.

the interface

From structure to surface

Research, ideation, and direction were shared across the team. Within that, I led the initial user flows, mapping how both families and advisors move through the two products, and I owned the consumer app’s UI, from the visual language through to the high-fidelity screens. Working across both the flows and the interface let me keep the experience consistent from structure to surface.

We moved quickly from low-fidelity wireframes into high-fidelity design. The design system stayed lightweight, enough to hold type, navigation, and shared components steady across both products, so what the project really shows is the final wireframes and the UI built from them.

Walkthrough of all features

Customer side: the banking app

Conversational onboarding

Setup opens with a voice-based AI assistant rather than a static form. It asks plain-language questions about who is in the household, how income arrives, and how expenses are shared, building a full picture whatever shape the family takes.

The adaptive dashboard

From that profile, the app generates a home screen built around the family’s real priorities. A single parent saving for a child sees something different from a couple managing an uneven mortgage.

Shared expenses sit at the center of it. The app reads income variation and suggests balanced ways to divide shared costs, breaks down each upcoming fixed payment, and tracks whether each person has paid their share, so the split, the schedule, and the current status all live in one view and nobody is left chasing money.

Community Bridge and the AI Advisor

Community Bridge lowers the barrier to getting help. Instead of hunting for someone who will understand their situation, families are connected directly to advisors who specialize in non-traditional family finance, and the same bridge appears on the advisor side, matching them with the households whose needs fit their expertise.

Alongside it, the AI Advisor handles the everyday questions. It explains financial concepts in plain language and helps families think through planning, so the information they need is available without booking anything. It is built to inform rather than to decide: it lays out options and what each one means, and the choice stays with the family. Anything carrying real weight is handed to a person through Community Bridge.

Institution side: the advisor software
AI-powered customer insights
Helps advisors understand varied, unique household structures quickly, so they can build solutions that fit the family in front of them.
Personalized financial offerings
Data-driven insight lets banks design customized programs and generate the legal documents non-traditional families often need.
Stronger client relationships
By closing gaps traditional banking leaves open, institutions build trust and engagement with a segment they usually miss.
Process

The full set of wireframes, mid-fidelity through high-fidelity.

The design went through two rounds of usability testing on UserTesting.com plus an in-class design review. Feedback on both the concept and the application UI was positive.

Case study · Branding / Packaging

NOTE — The Scent Lab

A rebrand rooted in place. A complete visual identity and packaging system for a Vietnamese fragrance house, turning the country’s cities and seasons into a language of scent and color.

Role
Graphic Designer, Brand Designer
Length
13 weeks
Tools
Adobe Illustrator, Adobe Photoshop
Scope
Brand strategy, logo and identity, packaging, and collateral
the idea

Scent as a sense of place

The concept is inspired by the richness and vibrant beauty of Vietnam’s landscapes, the birthplace of NOTE. Drawing on a country blessed by nature and steeped in cultural and historical heritage, the rebrand builds its imagery, symbols, and product names around famous landmarks that reflect the character of each region.

That decision, rooting every product in a specific place rather than an abstract mood, drove everything downstream: the naming, the color system, the imagery, and the way the collections are grouped. It also gave the brand a story that is specific to NOTE and hard to copy, which is exactly what a rebrand should protect.

identity

A mark that hides inside the O

The wordmark redraws NOTE in a custom high-contrast serif that reads as refined without feeling cold. The brand mark lives inside the letter O and folds three meanings into one shape:

  • Scent — a rising curved line, the trace of fragrance in the air.
  • Spark — a four-pointed star, the moment a candle is lit and a nod to the brand’s science-and-art spirit.
  • Candle — the spark set inside a circle, the product itself.
the collections

Named in two languages, rooted in place

The rebrand covers three lines, each named in both Vietnamese and English so the brand reads at home and abroad.

“Bắc Trung Nam” (North-Central-South) candle set
Three candles for three cities: Hà Nội Morning (Hà Nội Sớm), Streets of Hội An (Hội An Phố), and Sài Gòn Night (Sài Gòn Đêm), a north-to-south journey in three scents.
Đà Lạt Sunset (Đà Lạt Hoàng Hôn) diffuser
A single reed diffuser for the highland town, carrying the place-based idea into a second product type.
“Bốn Mùa” (Four Seasons) perfume set
Four perfumes, one per season, drawing on the country as a whole rather than a single city.
the design

A gradient for every place and time

Color is the core of the system. Each product gets a gradient that translates its place and time of day into a mood, so the palette is doing the same job as the fragrance:

  • Hà Nội Morning — fresh greens and bright yellows for early light. Notes of magnolia, lemon, and ginger.
  • Streets of Hội An — warm oranges and golds for the old town. Notes of jasmine, coconut, and green tea.
  • Sài Gòn Night — deep blues and purples for the city after dark. Notes of lavender, orange, and vanilla.
  • Đà Lạt Sunset — soft pinks and purples for the highland dusk. Notes of rose, pine, and pomelo.

For the perfumes, the same gradient logic maps onto the four seasons, from spring’s greens and pinks through to winter’s cool blues. Two recurring graphic devices hold the system together: simplified line-art of each city’s landmark (the pavilion at Hà Nội’s Temple of Literature, the covered bridge in Hội An, and the tower on the Sài Gòn skyline), and flower motifs rendered in the same gradients, drawn from the ingredients in each scent.

packaging

Color carries the box

On the packaging, the gradient fills the label, the landmark line-art sits as a quiet outline, and the type stays clean so color carries the identity. Consistent details keep the range coherent: the NOTE mark, the product type (“Nến thơm,” scented candle), the weight, and the scent notes.

The candle set ships in a pop-up box holding the three candle boxes and a thank-you card, so opening it is part of the experience rather than an afterthought.

The perfume set follows the same system in a four-bottle Bốn Mùa pack, and the Đà Lạt diffuser carries the concept into a boxed reed format.

collateral

One system across every touchpoint

To show the identity working as a brand rather than three labels, I extended it across a range of touchpoints: a 2024 product catalogue, thank-you cards and envelopes, digital billboards and lightbox displays, and roll-up banners for a launch. The same gradients, motifs, and type carry through all of them, which is where a system like this earns its keep.

Case study · Narrative design / AR

The Moon Drawer

Narrative design and AR storytelling for CyberTrophy. A 100-episode location-based narrative built into an existing AR game platform, where collecting a trophy becomes a moment in a story rather than a point on a scoreboard.

Lead visual designerResearch, ideation, and direction were shared across a team of three. I led creative direction and character and trophy concept development.
Length
25 weeks
Tools
Adobe Photoshop, Tripo 3D, Gemini, ChatGPT, Claude
Scope
Creative direction, narrative structure, character design, trophy concept development, AI-assisted visual production
Partner
CyberTrophy
Live in
CyberTrophy on the App Store →
CyberTrophy on Google Play →
01 — the opportunity

A collection loop with a ceiling

CyberTrophy is a Toronto-based AR app where players move through real-world locations to collect 3D trophies. The platform had five stories in its library, all built on the same underlying logic: go somewhere, complete an objective, earn a reward, repeat.

The mechanics work. Movement-based games are effective at getting people outside and engaged with their surroundings. But the loop has a ceiling. Once the novelty of collecting wears off, there’s nothing pulling a player back. There is no reason to care about what they’ve collected beyond the fact that they collected it.

That gap was the opportunity. A location-based game already has something most digital experiences don’t: physical presence. The player is somewhere real, moving through actual space. If a story could attach itself to that movement, if arriving somewhere unlocked a moment rather than just a checkpoint, the collection loop stops being a mechanic and starts being an experience.

The brief we set for ourselves: build a complete narrative layer inside CyberTrophy’s existing infrastructure that makes trophies feel like something worth keeping.

02 — the approach

A story you can play while walking

The story came first. The narrative direction had to answer one question: what kind of story fits a game you play while walking? That gave us three design considerations:

  • Episodes needed to be short, readable in the moment, mid-walk.
  • The tone needed to be gentle and accessible, not demanding emotional heavy lifting from someone opening the app casually.
  • The story had to work without asking the player to type anything.

The world that emerged: a fading cottage of memories. The player wakes with no clear sense of self. Inside, a mysterious drawer, the Moon Drawer, reveals objects from their past. Each object becomes a trophy representing a memory, a feeling, or a piece of identity. As objects accumulate on the Memory Shelf, the world regains warmth and color. But time and pressure begin to distort things. Memories glitch. Doubt creeps in.

Core idea: remembering yourself in a world that tries to make you forget.

Narrative structure

100 episodes across 10 chapters, each built around a distinct emotional theme: comfort, intuition, pressure, slowing down, memory, habit.

Pacing was deliberate. The first thirty episodes stay in gentle territory: favourite places, comforting memories, familiar foods, childhood locations, objects kept but rarely used. The story eases players in before it asks anything harder. Each episode introduces one symbolic object tied to that moment’s emotional register. The trophy is what the player collects, the memory is what they carry.

Rather than a fictional protagonist, the player is the main character. Their movement, their memories, their interpretations are what the story runs on. Meaning emerges from what they bring rather than what they’re told.

Character design

Two characters carry the story’s central tension.

The Fox

Intuition · emotional truth · safety
Form
A chibi upright figure: bright orange fur, white shirt, blue suspenders, light bow tie.
Presence
Soft and calm, never forcing the player anywhere.
Iteration
Early versions were more realistic and read as ambiguous rather than warm. The final form had to be immediately legible as gentle, even at small scale in an AR interface.

The Clockmaker

Pressure · judgment · productivity anxiety
Form
A shadowy figure with a flat square clock for a head.
Why that shape
Round clocks read as friendly and grandfather clocks read as whimsical. A flat rectangular face reads as mechanical and slightly wrong, which is exactly what the character needed.
Arc
Softens across the story, mirroring the player’s own movement from external validation toward self-understanding.

Together they externalize a tension most people recognize without needing it explained.

Visual direction

The aesthetic was set early through a moodboard: cottage-core, soft, nostalgic, whimsical. Warm and unhurried. All-ages-friendly without being childish.

Characters and symbolic objects were sketched digitally in Adobe Photoshop before any other production began. Each trophy was worked out from an emotional brief rather than a visual one: what physical form does a memory take, what shape does slowing down make.

Production approach

Producing 100 unique cover art images required a workflow decision.

The first approach was fully hand-illustrated. Following a conversation with CyberTrophy about audience reception of AI-generated art, the team explored whether the visual set could be produced entirely through traditional digital illustration. The team worked with this approach for a period, but the volume of output required made it unworkable at scale.

The move to AI-assisted production followed CyberTrophy’s existing workflow, since the company already used generative tools in its visual pipeline. That precedent reframed the question from whether to use AI to how to use it well. As production scaled, we integrated AI tools to accelerate rendering, but with strict creative control. AI served as:

  • A refinement tool, not an author
  • A way to generate visual variations from finished sketches
  • A method for exploring symbolic ambiguity
  • A pipeline aligned with CyberTrophy’s 3D rendering system

The production ran on a repeating six-stage cycle, with the full loop running three times before the visual system stabilized.

01Ideation

Developing the concept based on the partner brief.

02Make

Sketching, writing, prompting, generating.

03Document

Logging prompts, outputs, errors, variations.

04Reflect

Analyzing ambiguity and authorial intent.

05Refine

Adjusting prompts, curating outputs.

06Apply

Integrating visuals and narrative into the app.

Three full loops before the visual system stabilized.

03 — the product

Live as CyberTrophy’s sixth story

What was built:

  • A complete 100-episode narrative across 10 chapters
  • 100 original symbolic trophy designs, each tied to a specific episode and emotional theme
  • 100 unique episode cover art images
  • Two original characters with full visual and narrative development
  • Full integration into CyberTrophy’s existing AR platform

How it works in the app: The player opens the Moon Drawer, a mechanic that signals willingness to engage, and receives an episode. The trophy appears in AR, anchored to wherever they physically are. Once collected, it goes on the Memory Shelf, a space that accumulates and shifts as the story progresses. No text input required. The reflection happens through interpretation.

The trophies don’t just reward movement. They mean something.

What it demonstrates: Narrative depth and game mechanics aren’t in tension. A location-based platform can carry a story that asks something of its players, not a quest to complete, but a memory to sit with.

The 3D models were rendered by the CyberTrophy development team from the original 2D designs, completing the translation from sketch to AR object.

04 — reflection

Reflection as an interaction mode

The Moon Drawer shows that reflection can be an interaction mode in AR gaming, not an add-on. By combining narrative identity principles, symbolic objects, and AI-assisted visual variation, we built a movement-based story that gives players a reason to pause and interpret their own experience, all within CyberTrophy’s existing platform, without new infrastructure.

The AI process taught me something I didn’t expect going in. Used with clear authorial control, generative tools expanded both what I could explore creatively and how much I could produce. The judgment about what fits a story world is still design work. It just looks different than drawing.

School project · Fabrication

No Swimming

A swimming pool in a box, built to learn six machines I had never touched before.

Scope
Concept, layout planning, fabrication
Tools
Adobe Illustrator, Rhino, RhinoCAM, Bambu Studio
Made with
Plywood, foam, aluminum, acrylic, PLA
the brief

Six processes, one object

The assignment was to build one object using a set list of materials and machines. Wood, metal, foam, a 3D print, something laser cut, something UV printed. Six processes I had never used before, and a real risk of ending up with six unrelated things glued together.

So I started from the materials instead of the concept. I looked for a subject where each material would already have an obvious job, and a swimming pool covered most of them. The wooden box is the pool, the metal is the ladder, and the foam mills easily into a wavy water surface. The acrylic became the smaller things you’d find at a pool, a life ring and a sign.

All together, I wanted to make something fun and whimsical: a whale in a swimming pool!

planning

Laying it out flat first

Before I touched a machine, I laid out every side of the box at real size in Adobe Illustrator. I could check the proportions, settle the colours early, and pull a full material list straight off the artboard before cutting anything. A lot of the problems I avoided later came from doing that first.

how it came together

Crafting!

Making came second, and that was six unfamiliar machines in a row, testing on offcuts and adjusting as each material behaved differently from the drawing.

The box came first. I cut the plywood panels on the table saw, then clamped and glued them up and sanded the joints back before any colour went on. The ladder followed: cutting the aluminum to length and bending it by hand, which is where holding the spare pieces up against the box earned its keep.

The water surface started as a model in Rhino. I set the toolpaths in RhinoCAM, milled it out of foam on the MultiCam, and finished it with acrylic paint. The smaller pool pieces were laser cut from acrylic, and the life ring went through the Ricoh flatbed with a white underbase and double-sided registration so it reads the same from either side of the box. The whale tail was a 3D print, rescaled and prepped in Bambu Studio.

Finishing pulled it together: paint, sanding, and final assembly, with the milled foam dropped onto its blocks last so the water sits level.
